## Introduction 
The aim of this experiment was to qualitatively determine the extent to which various classes of sunscreen block UV rays from passing through, and thereby demonstrate the extent to which it protects human skin and DNA from damage. This was demonstrated by using yeast that is very sensitive to UV light, allowing for visualisation of UV penetration by observing the extent and location of its growth. 
## Methods
In this experiment, an agar plate was smeared with yeast and the top and bottom plates taped together. The plate was then divided into four quadrants using a marker on the underside of the bottom plate. On the top plate, each quadrant was covered with a different substance: F15 sunscreen, F30 sunscreen, paper, and one control quadrant left with no covering at all.

After that, the agar plate and yeast were treated with UV light of wavelength 254 nanometers and 500,000 microjoules, then incubated at 30 degrees Celsius for two days. 
## Results
In the final results, yeast can be seen growing in the quadrants that were covered with sunscreen and paper, whereas there was no yeast to be seen in the control quadrant. 

The yeast also grew significantly more in both the sunscreen quadrants compared to that of the paper quadrant, and there was more growth in the F30 quadrant compared to the F15 quadrant. 
## Discussion
As expected, the control quadrant had no yeast growing in it as there was nothing to block it from the UV light there. The difference in extent of growth in the different quadrants also seem to indicate that the sunscreen, both F15 and F30, was able to block UV light more effectively than the paper, and that F30 sunscreen was able to block UV more effectively than the F15 sunscreen. 

However, the disparity between the growth in the F30 and F15 quadrants may be due to small size of the quadrants and UV light shining from the empty section of the covering into the other quadrants, affecting growth in those sections. This can be seen in the growth of the yeast in the sections of the F15 and paper quadrants directly adjacent to the control quadrant, where there is no yeast growth. Conversely, the F30 quadrant is diagonally opposite the control quadrant and therefore has less area potentially affected. 

One improvement to this experiment would be to separate the four quadrants into individual agar plates to prevent such interference in the future, as well as to cover the sides of the agar plate to prevent UV light from shining in from unprotected areas.
