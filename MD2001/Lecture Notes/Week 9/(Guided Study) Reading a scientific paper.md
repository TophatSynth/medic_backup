Optimal timing of influenza vaccination in young children: population based cohort study. CM Worsham, CF Bray, AB Jena and JP Newhouse BMJ 2024;384:e077076

1. What is the research question?
	- What is the optimal timing of influenza vaccination in young children 
2. What is the study population?
    - Commercially insured children aged 2-5 years in the US who were born between August 1 and January 31, and were vaccinated against influenza during 2011-18.
3. How many participants were in the study?
    - 819,223 
4. How was infection with influenza measured?
	- The presence of at least one insurance claim for an ambulatory, emergency department, or inpatient visit containing any of several ICD-9 or ICD-10 influenza diagnosis codes or a prescription claim for oseltamivir
5. Do you think this is an appropriate measure to answer the research question?
	- It omits children that had a hospital visit due to influenza that did not have insurance claims submitted, or children that contracted influenza but didn't go to the hospital. Overall, though, this would be a very small minority of patients, so I think it is an appropriate measure. 
6. Review Figure 2. What type of graph is this?
    - Cumulative probability graph
7. What is the main result shown in figure 2?
    - Children born in August are much more likely to be vaccinated earlier in August-October, than children born in October and December. However, almost all children were vaccinated by the end of December. 
8. What proportion of children with October birthdays were vaccinated in October and how does this compare with those born in November? 
    - October: 48.9%
    - November: 27.3% 
9. Review figure 3. What is plotted on the x axis?
    - Birth month
10. What is the range in percentage of children that were diagnosed as having influenza?
    - 0.4% 
11. Which birth month has the lowest % influenza diagnosis?
    - October
12. Fig 3 shows unadjusted and adjusted rates of influenza diagnosis. One of the things that there is adjustment for is medical comorbidities of the child. Why might this be important?
    - Comorbidities may result in an immunocompromised system that may increase the chances of an influenza infection. 
13. Taking the results from figure 2 and 3 together what do you conclude?
    - Children born in October are vaccinated earlier, which helps prevent influenza infections 
14. What is an important assumption in this study?
    - Early vaccinations prevent infections 
15. Identify one potential problem with the study.
    - Correlation doesn't mean causation

18. Overall how likely do you think this paper is to influence future clinical practice and why?
	- I think it is relatively likely to influence future clinical practice, as it is based on good data and demonstrates a beneficial effect of a relatively small change in vaccination date. On the other hand, having a separate appointment for an influenza vaccination leads to more work for healthcare workers, so I am unsure as to the extent that this will influence their clinical practice, considering the NHS is already quite overworked and under-resourced.
