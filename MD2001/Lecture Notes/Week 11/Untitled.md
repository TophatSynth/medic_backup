Misogyny in Medicine: Critically evaluate the impact of ‘misogyny in medicine’, and the strategies being proposed to address this. 

Misogyny is a major societal issue faced across disciplines; in medicine, it manifests as the unambiguous gender bias experienced in Western medical systems (Greenhalgh, 2022). The danger of this prejudice in a medical diagnosis persists.  For instance, in the case of Michelle, who was suffering from bloody bowel movements that were ‘all in her head’ for 12 years before her sixth doctor diagnosed her with irritable bowel syndrome and ulcerative colitis following a colonoscopy (Criado-Perez, 2019). Her experiences, as accounted in Criado-Perez's book, illustrate an example of gender bias and everyday systemic discrimination women face in medicine. This failure of the healthcare system is the manifestation of misogyny through deep-rooted historical values. A core value paving the route is andronormativity, defined as the unilateral consideration of the male body and emotions as the norm in medicine (Samulowitz et al., 2018). It governs diagnostic symptoms by implying women fall within the natural distribution of male subjects, a generalisation made irrespective of conflicting modern literature. This contributes to the gender data gap in the under-representation of women in medical research- a cascading effect (Greenhalgh, 2022). I believe the rise and persistence of misogyny needs to be acknowledged openly and pushed towards reform to prevent the endangerment of women’s diagnosis, treatment and health outcomes. This essay will analyse the historical roots of medical misogyny, then explore the impact on patient care and professional lives, before concluding with an assessment of proposed strategic solutions. 

The contemporary crisis of misogyny in medicine is not an anomaly, but a legacy of long-standing beliefs that have systematically positioned the male body as the default. For instance, Hippocrates, the father of medicine, legitimised the diagnosis of hysteria in women - a sickness inducing ‘wandering uterus’ which justified abnormal symptoms or an influx of emotions seen in female patients (Greenhalgh, 2022). This is important as it rendered female anatomy problematic and framed women as inherently unstable, simply due to their reproductive organs. Furthermore, in ancient Greece, women were described by Aristotle as the ‘incomplete’ man (Criado-Perez, 2019). Despite the correction of Aristotelian views, this historical differentiation bore the consequences of setting the universal standard seen today as the male body.  

Hence, the patriarchal foundation of medical knowledge, based on the male norm, persists, as evidenced by curricula differentiated teaching of ‘physiology’ and ‘female physiology’, or the representation of male images three times as frequently as female images in neutral body parts, as noted by Criado-Perez (2019). Consequently, building seemingly informed biases in healthcare work as medically factual, setting the stage for systemic corruption against women. Despite conflicting literature emphasising sex as a major determinant of health outcomes, textbooks continue failing to report sex-specific information because researchers fail to acknowledge a difference (Criado-Perez, 2019; Leslie, 2020). This false universal leads to the gender data gap in research, which has perpetuated a lack of treatment efficiency for females. However, in recent years, legislative policies such as the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) Revitalization Act 1993 have encouraged the inclusion of women by enforcing the reporting of health outcomes by sex (Leslie, 2020). While a significant step towards gender equality, change must be radical, as guidelines for clinical trials continue to collapse the general requirement of representation without the exclusive mention of sex. This systemic failure is detrimental to women’s health, causing misdiagnosis and mistreatment due to their ‘atypical’ bodies and disease patterns neglected in research.  

The ingrained androcentrism directly translates into tangible harm for female patients, manifesting as misdiagnosis, dismissal of pain, and treatments developed from unreliable data. For example, the perception of a heart attack as a male disease, even though economically disadvantaged women are 25% more likely to suffer from heart disease than their male counterparts (Criado-Perez, 2019). The author places emphasis on the deaths being a consequence of misdiagnosis due to their uncharacteristic symptoms of stomach pain, fatigue, and nausea as opposed to chest and left-arm pains. This reveals the reality of a woman visiting a doctor, a majority admitting their lack of confidence in treating sex-tailored diseases, explaining the high mortality rates in cardiovascular causes. 

The statistics reinforce the idea of women presenting symptoms being overlooked or pushed aside as emotional, which is known as Yentl syndrome. This links in with social stigma in pain literature, which supports women’s increased pain sensitivity and their expression of that pain as compared to men (Samulowitz et al., 2018). Going on to describe the social stigma of men needing to appear as enduring, whereas societal expectations are to speak out about pain. Nevertheless, physicians refuse to listen to and believe in their chronic pain, falling into the hysteria trap and continuing to build up toxic hegemonic masculinity with an inconclusive diagnosis (Samulowitz et al., 2018). 

Beyond the clinic, misogyny creates a hostile professional environment that systematically disadvantages female doctors, impacting career progression, mental health, and compromising safety. In Australian medicine, Chueng (2023) mentions a range of discrimination in the form of microaggression, harassment, and sexual assault. Daily experiences include sexist jokes in teaching spaces, underappreciation in discussions, harassment by patients and colleagues, and lower income (Kang & Kaplan, 2019). This double standard is unsurprising, given the historical infrastructure of medicine and is a contribution to their professional attrition. The structural inequality is shown by the invisible workload carried by female staff who are more likely to provide mentoring, patient-centred, and community service (Chueng, 2023). Irrespective of this, the gender pay gap (12-25% lower than males across fields) persists, and men continue to occupy more leadership positions that dictate their employees’ careers, therefore further discouraging women from advocating against mistreatment. The accumulation of these factors based on sex inequality develops into imposter syndrome, burnout, and a lack of fulfilment in their chosen profession (Chueng, 2023). Ultimately, this causes a stunted career that results in quitting, a critical loss to the already understaffed hospitals, and quality of public health. The professional environment actively pushes out competent women, not only harming careers but also the healthcare system of talent that demonstrably improves patient care. 

In response to the predisposition of women in health, proposed strategies are evolving from attempts to ‘fix the women’ toward comprehensive, systemic interventions aimed at redesigning the structure of medical practice and research.  This can be seen in advice and interventions women receive to act more like men in posture, assertiveness and negotiation techniques (Kang & Kaplan, 2019). However, this process is counterintuitive and unproductive; it fails to tackle the root systemic environment and places increased stress on women to mould into a toxic role model against their better instincts. Additionally, Kang & Kaplan (2019) acknowledge the negative repercussions faced when adopting these outdated solutions, including violating feminine social norms and being at higher risk for punishments. Hence, leading us to advocate for systemic reform against misogyny in medicine. Addressing the gender data gap created by androcentric research beliefs by supporting the proposal of compulsory inclusion of women in trials across journals, hospital boards, universities, and government (Criado-Perez, 2019; Leslie, 2020). Leslie (2020) stresses the condition as a prerequisite for grants to shut down resistance in response to the cost and variability of testing on women. Beyond this, the enforcement of sex-segregated reporting and data analysis to account for every differing component to maximise data resources and treat women to the right standard. 

The subsequent step in systemic change is to introduce an organisational accountability framework at all levels. For instance, targeted goals or the Athena SWAN charter that address misogyny and call for advancing the careers of women in STEM (Kang & Kaplan, 2019; Chueng, 2023). This shift will promote the necessary organisational shift and work in supplement to raising awareness on an individual level and steer the healthcare system to achieve equality. The focus will be on tangible, transparent and measurable results to be tailored as mandates for group change. This ties into action plans that outline definitive steps to ensure the goal is met as opposed to baseless milestones such as achieving an equal ratio of men and women on committees within a set time frame (Kang & Kaplan, 2019). The addition of women in leadership roles to abolish discriminatory behaviour seen in andromorphic surgery culture (Chueng, 2023). This will signal a new safe culture by answering the call for women in executive positions from the top-bottom rewriting the social environment in hospitals. Equally, the transition will encourage speaking up against gender bias, a crucial mechanism to allow continuous feedback from all stakeholders. All in all, while awareness training has its place, the most promising strategies are those that change the rules of the system itself, making bias harder to enact and equity the default path. 

This essay has critically evaluated misogyny through the lenses of its historical clauses and revealed it to be a deep-rooted systemic issue with detrimental consequences for both patients and professionals. It went on to expand the disparity amongst practitioners and advocated for revolutionising institutional polices and structures. The impact of misogyny is a healthcare system that is less effective, less safe, and less equitable for women. The literature recommends advocacy for women in leadership, transforming the structural foundation through mandatory result-oriented aims to continuously adjust strategies that support gender equality. The battle against misogyny is a cornerstone of achieving gender equality in healthcare. The cost of negligence is women's livelihoods and lives at stake, calling to action policymakers and institutions globally.
