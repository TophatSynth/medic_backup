In this session, each of our group members talked individually to a simulated patient with an elbow injury that seemed to be related to a tennis tournament. We were all really nervous before the consultation as this was the first time interacting with a patient one on one for most of us. Despite this, I felt that we all did quite well in the end, with good flow of questions and empathetic verbal and non-verbal communication. Personally I was quite happy with my performance as I managed to get quite far into the consultation before the time ran out — I was able to get to the patient's medical and family history, where a lot of my group only got up to the presenting complaint. 

However, upon reviewing the video, I realised that during the first half of the session, I was leaning forward excessively, to the point of looking like a shrimp. This is because my default instinct when trying to look empathetic is to lean forward and maintain eye contact to show interest and attention. However, in my nervousness during this session, I had inadvertently leaned forward too far to the point of looking slightly ridiculous. Fortunately, around halfway into the session, I did readjust my posture, though I didn't realise its extent until later. I feel that this is an important point to address, as my body language plays an integral part in the conversation, and in extension, my relationship with the patient. If my body posture is off, it can make the patient feel awkward and less likely to trust me, and therefore curb the effectiveness of the history taking. 

During the consultation, I was very nervous and I felt that translated into a lot of stuttering and waffling as I talked. Upon review of the video, however, I realised that those problems weren't nearly as bad as I had thought at first. I still need to work on being more concise when asking questions, but not to the extent that I had thought at the time. This goes to show that my perception of an experience and my performance can be affected significantly by nerves and anxiety — a sentiment echoed by many of my group members, who all mentioned that they thought they paused for too long during the session, only to find out that the pause was much shorter in reality. 

For further communication workshops, I intend to work on remembering to check and be aware of my posture and body language during the consultation. I will do this by intentionally periodically mentally checking in on my posture as the session continues, and also practising this during my everyday conversations with friends and classmates. I also intend to work on being nervous by reminding myself that, despite feeling that I did poorly during the consultation, the results may be wildly different in reality, without the hyper-awareness that comes with nervousness. This is a very important thing to remember not only for the next communications exercise, but also for my upcoming exams and OSCE's.
