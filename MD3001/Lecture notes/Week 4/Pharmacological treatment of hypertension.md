When to treat? 
- Stage 1 hypertension with history of CVD, renal disease, diabetes, 10 year CV risk of >= 10% → treat
	- Otherwise, monitor BP and recommend lifestyle modifications 
		- Eg weight loss, reduce salt intake (<6g/day - damn, sad.), 
- Stage 2 - just treat

Therapeutics 
- ACE inhibitors 
- Angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB's)
- Diuretics
- Calcium channel blockers (CCB's)
- β1-adrenergic receptor blockers
- α1-adrenergic receptor blockers

- Step 1 - if <55 years old or only stage 1 HPT → (A) ACE inhibitor

BP targets
- ![[Pasted image 20261008121919.png|284]]
- Usually <140/90 clinic BP and <135/85 ABPM/HBPM 
- >80 yo - <150/90 clinic BP and <145/85 ABPM/HBPM

Effect of angiotensin II 
- ![[Pasted image 20261008122641.png|338]]
- Binds to AT1 receptors 
	- Stimulates Na absorption in the proximal convoluted tubule in the kidneys, and stimulates aldosterone release → further Na reabsorption in the cortical collecting duct (CCD) ![[Pasted image 20261008123006.png|316]]
- ACE inhibitors
	- Prevents production of angiotensin II → reduced Na reabsorption
	- HOWEVER! ACE is involved in kinin-kallikrein system - kallikrein cleaves kininogen into bradykinin - vasodilator
		- Bradykinin is a vasodilator
		- ACE breaks bradykinin down - ACE inhibited → buildup of vasodilator → decrease in BP ← reduced Na reabsorption 
- ARB's 
	- Blocks angiotensin II binding to AT1 - same effect as ACE inhibitor but doesn't affect bradykinin 

Diuretics
- Helps body get rid of water - targets Na absorption 
- Reduces blood pressure by decreasing ECV 
	1. Loop diuretics 
		- Most powerful class of diuretic ("torrential urine flow") 
		- Inhibits the NKCC2 (Na/K/2Cl cotransporter) in thick ascending limb 
		- Furosemide 
		- Only usually used in treatment of HPT when renal function is impaired 
	2. Thiazide diuretics
		- Less powerful 
		- Inhibits the NCC (Na/Cl cotransporter) in the distal convoluted tubule 
		- Indapamide
		- 2nd/3rd line treatment 
	3. K sparing diuretics 
		- Limited diurectic action when given alone, but very powerful when used in combination with the other two diuretic types 
		- Inhibits ENaC (Epithelial Na channel) in the cortical collecting duct 
		- 2 types
			- Direct inhibitor - amiloride 
			- Aldosterone antagonists (aka mineralocorticoid antagonists) - spironolactone 
		- Rarely used due to also blocking K excretion - causes hyperkalaemia 
			- K excretion is coupled to ENaC 

Ca channel blockers 
- Inhibits contraction in the heart and vascular smooth muscle by targetting L-type voltage gated Ca channels
- ![[Pasted image 20261008124532.png|289]]![[Pasted image 20261008124542.png|285]]![[Pasted image 20261008124550.png|283]]![[Pasted image 20261008124601.png|288]]![[Pasted image 20261008124610.png|312]]![[Pasted image 20261008124622.png|327]]
- ![[Pasted image 20261008124635.png|453]]
- 2 types
	- Non-dihydropyridines 
		- Not really used anymore
	- Dihydropyridines
		- Amlodipine 
- Effect 
	- Cardiac - decreased contractility, HR, and conduction velocity 
	- Smooth muscle - decreased coronary artery and peripheral vessel constriction 
	- Both contribute to decreased BP 

β1 antagonists
- Non selective β1 antagonists have side effects of bronchoconstrction ← important in asthmatics
- β1 selective antagonists 
	- Decreases HR/force of contraction → decrease CO and BP 
	- Also inhibits renin release → decrease Ang II 
	- Bisoprolol
	- So why not first line treatment? 
		- Less effective in reducing CV and diabetes risk 
		- Less well tolerated than ACE inhibtitors/ARB's 
	- But still useful for HPT with additional needs for β-blockade, eg angina or HF

α1 antagonists
- Non selective antagonists side effects - postural hypotension, reflex tachycardia 
- Selective antagonists - decreases vasoconstriction → decreased BP 
	- Doxazosin 
	- Modest effects compared to ACE inhibitors/ARB's/CCB's 
	- Used as 4th line treatment, after assessment of patient
