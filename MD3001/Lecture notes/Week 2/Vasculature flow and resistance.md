LaPlace's law 
- Distending pressure (P) produces an opposing force or tension (T) in the vessel wall, proportional to the radius (R) of the vessel 
	- ![[Pasted image 20260921092102.png]]
	- Tension = Pressure * radius 
	- Therefore in aorta, with large pressure and radius, you would need a huuuuuuge amount of smooth muscle to generate the necessary tension to remain static - so we just ┐(ツ)┌ don't 
	- Once you get to arterioles, you have small enough radius that the tension required is low enough that you can have smooth muscle to occlude the vessel
	- Capillaries can then be extremely thin and still withstand pressure due to their small radius (the pressure is lower due to larger net radius but whatever) 
	- Consequences for aneurysms 
		- ![[Pasted image 20260921092639.png]]
		- Weakening of the vessel wall → ballooning of the wall in that part → increase in pressure in that area due to increased radius → balloons further → burst :D 

Regulation of blood flow  
- Arterioles + precapillary sphincters 
	- SNS control to close spincters and decrease flow 
	- Local cells can signal to precapillary spincters to increase blood flow
		- Factors 
			- Active/reactive hyperemia - key local factors associated with metabolic activity of tissues
				- Active hyperemia - when tissue is highly active, blood flow increases - eg up to 20x in skm ![[Pasted image 20260921093543.png]]
				- Reactive hyperemia - when blood supply is blocked, blood flow then increases to 4-7x normal flow once blood flow is restored![[Pasted image 20260921093627.png]]
			- Vasomotion - spontaneous contraction and dilation of the blood vessels - random perfusion just to keep things moving about
			- Response to injury - endothelin-1 is released form endothelial cells when damaged - potent vasoconstrictor 
			- Flow autoregulation 
				- Arterioles constrict and dilate when arterial pressure increases and decreases respectively - protects capillaries
				- Constriction is a myogenic response - stretch-activated Ca channels get activated → influx of Ca → constriction 
	- Other factors
		- Nitrous Oxide - key modulator of vascular radius![[Pasted image 20260921093711.png]]
			- Abnormal shear stress in the endothelial cells → generation of NO from eNOS 
			- NO as a gas diffuses easily across into the smooth muscle, activates a soluble guanylate cyclase - converts cGTP into cGMP → causes relaxation 
			- Happens as a local process in all of your vasculature, but NO-releasing nerves (PSNS) are present only in your genitalia 
		- Others![[Pasted image 20260921093905.png]]
			- Decreased oxygen, increased K/CO2/H, bradykinin 
			- Adrenaline normally causes vasoconstriction, other than in skeletal muscle - causes vasodilation there for more blood/oxygen
			- Adenosine is a very strong vasodilator - released locally - needs to overpower things like noradrenaline from neural signals so that tissues won't be cut off from blood by SNS
				- Adenosine is a breakdown product of ATP - use a lot of ATP → adenosine is generated → helps increase blood flow when tissue is working hard
- Metarterioles - bypass capillaries to venules so when the precapillary sphincters constrict, the blood has somewhere to go

- EXCEPTIONS
	- Skin 
		- ![[Pasted image 20260921095007.png|243]]
		- Arteriovenous anastomosis/shunts - allows blood to not flow towards epidermis to maintain body heat 
	- Lungs
		- Decreased oxygen perfusion in a region causes decreased blood flow to that region
		- Mediator unknown :/ 
	- Kidneys
		- ![[Pasted image 20260921095304.png|127]]
		- Usually arteriole → capillary → venule
		- Circulation in glomerulus goes arteriole → capillary in bowman's capsule → arteriole 
		- Arterioles have decent amount of smooth muscle that allows them to precisely control the pressure across the glomerulus - venules do not
