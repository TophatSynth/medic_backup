# Overview of neurotransmission at the NMJ ![[Pasted image 20260202111343.png]]
- Ach -> nicotinic Ach receptor -> Ach degraded very quickly after
- Feedback mechanism for when a large signal is needed - Little bit of Ach released -> feedback excitatory -> more Ach released - ACh broken down by ACh.E really fast -> ends the feedback mechanism by virtue of no ACh left
- 3 main ways
	- Presynaptically - inhibiting ACh synthesis in the neuron - choline uptake
	- Presynaptically - inhibiting ACh release - prevent fusion of vesicles and release of NT
	- Postsynaptically - preventing effects of ACh on muscle - intefering with receptor binding

# Presynaptic blockade
## Inhibiting ACh release ![[Pasted image 20260202111902.png]]
- LA's and general anaesthetics 
- Inhibitors/competitors of calcium - Mg ions and some antibiotics (tetracycline) -> inhibit vesicle fusion
- Neurotoxins - Botulinum toxin (Botox, from clostridium botulinum), Beta-bungarotoxin (from snakes)
	- Botox - can also be used for muscle spasticity - also a treatment for hyperhydrosis (Botox inhibits ACh release - ACh triggers sweat glands -> inhibition stops excessive sweating)

## Clinical use for NMJ-blocking drugs
- Endotracheal intubation + mechanical ventilation
- Surgical procedures 
	- To allow surgical access to abdominal cavity - sometimes GA's aren't enough to relax the abdominal muscles - ↓ concentration of general anaesthetic needed 
	- To ensure immobility (e.g. prevent cough during head and neck surgery)
	- Allow relaxation to reduce displaced fracture or dislocation
- In conjunction with electroconvulsive therapy - so the muscles aren't twitching

# Postsynaptic blockade
- Nicotinic ACh receptor
	- Activated by 2 ACh molecules binding to it -> opens up to allow Na and K to pass through 
- Receptor agonists and antagonists
	- Agonists - activates the receptor -> open
	- Antagonist - competitive, keeps receptor from being bound -> stays closed 
- Blockers 
	- Non-depolarising blockers ![[Pasted image 20260202113735.png]]
		- Competitive antagonists of Nicotinic ACh receptors
		- Eg atracurium, mivacurium, pancuronium, vecuronium, rocuronium
		- ![[Pasted image 20260202113925.png]]
		- Why is tubocurarine not used anymore? - significantly blocks ganglions, leading to hypotension (bad for patients with myocardial ischaemia)
	- Depolarising blockers 
		- Agonists of nicotinic ACh receptors 
		- Not metabolised by ACh esterase - so inital depolarisation (-> some contraction of muscle fibres), but stays bound to the receptor for longer -> prolonged motor depolarisation of the muscle membrane -> cannot reset the voltage gated sodium channels  bc the membrane potential is still too high ([[Ionic basis of the action potential#The action potential|needs the potential to drop to reset]]) -> no more muscle action potentials ![[Pasted image 20260202114404.png]]
		- eg suxamethonium
		- 2 phases
			- Phase 1 - still safe
				- Muscle fasciculations then contraction blocked
					- Fasciculations - involuntary rapid muscle twitches that are too weak to move a limb but can be felt by patients and seen or palpated by clinicians
				- Repolarisation blocked 
				- Na channels kept inactivated 
			- Phase 2 - too long or too high dose
				- Desensitisation blockade - depolarisation cannot occur even once drug is gone <- seems to be a mild conformational change that takes a while to reverse

# Restoring transmission
## Metabolism and elimination of NMJ blockers 
- Ester hydrolysis and Hofmann elimination ([E2 elimination](https://www.masterorganicchemistry.com/2017/10/18/the-hofmann-elimination/)) - Atracurium
- Plasma cholinesterases - mivacurium, suxamethonium
	- Can have very variable effects - it wears off v fast for some ppl, lasts for hours in others 
- Hepatic metabolism - pancuronium, vecuronium
	- Don't use in patients with bad liver 
- Passed out in bile/urine - rocuronium

### Anticholineseterase drugs
- Types of cholineseterases
	- Acetylcholinesterases (ACh.E) 
		- "True" colinesterase - specific for hydroysis of ACh
		- Present in the conducting tissue as well as RBC's
		- Bound to the basal lamina in the synaptic cleft
	- Plasma cholinesterases 
		- Pseudocholinesterase - acts on a broad spectrum of substrates 
		- Soluble in plasma and is found like, everywhere
- Inhibitors of cholinesesterase enzymes ![[Pasted image 20260202115421.png]]
	- Irreversible - not clinically used. poison. bad. 
	- Reversible - very slowly hydrolysed
	- Organophosphates used in herbicides  
- Side effects of anticholinesterases (why are there so many...)
	- CNS - unconsciousness/resp failure
	- ANS - lots of involuntary stuff
- Clinical uses
	- Anaesthesia - reverse non-depolarising muscle blockade, or to counteract PSNS effects 
		- Blocke ACh.E's -> ACh is not broken down -> can then compete with the blockers more -> restore transmission 
	- Treating [[Neurotransmitters#Neurotransmitters|Myasthenia Gravis]] - increased amount of ACh -> increased transmission  
	- Treating glaucoma (optical nerve damaged, usually from increased intraocular pressure -> vision loss if untreated) - facilitates the drainage of aqueous humour -> lower the intraocular pressure
- Selective relaxant binding agent (SRBA)
	- Binds to the anticholinesterase -> rapidly reverse the effects
