You are a medical student currently attached to the A&E Department of your hospital. An ambulance crew bring in a young man from a road accident. You listen to the crew handing the patient over to the doctors and nurses.
- Already thinking about trauma, neuro/cranial damage, internal/external bleeding, broken/fractured bones, respiratory status

The patient is Vasile Popescu, age 19 years. He was riding his motorbike in the rain after finishing work when, according to an eye witness, he lost control in the rain and slid with his bike across the road. His left shoulder apparently impacted first against the kerb. He was found by the ambulance crew on the pavement with his left arm under his damaged motorbike. The crew also state that they “immobilised his C-spine with a collar” and that he has been complaining of severe pain in his left arm.
- Road rash 
- Impacted first against the kerb - [[Nerves of the upper limb#^e1269d|potential upper brachial plexus injury]], broken clavicle and maybe some ribs, potentially but unlikely to have dislocated shoulder 
- Left arm under motorbike - impinging on nerves/blood vessel -> ischemia 
	- Coupled with most likely some external bleeding - even worse
- C-spine - cervical spine 
	- [Whiplash](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/whiplash-injury) - damage to muscle, disks, nerves, tendons
	- Immobilised to prevent further damage on top of potential injury
- Pain in his left arm
	- Broken something probably 
- Complaining - cognitive functional

You watch as one the nurses write down his initial observations:-
Airway – Speaking
Breathing – RR 22 breaths/minute.
Circulation – HR 115/minute
BP 130/82 mmHg
Saturation 98% on air
- Trachea undamaged, brain mostly alright - only mild/moderate damage 
- Tachypnea/tachycardia - pain, scared
- BP - within normal range - no hypovolemic shock yet
- SpO2 - okay
- Injury likely mostly limited to upper limb 

One of the doctors inserts an intravenous cannula into Vasile’s right arm, starts IV fluids and gives him an injection of morphine, reassuring the patient that this will help the pain. The doctor asks you if you are familiar with the term ‘pain-ladder’. 
- Morphine IV - Initially 5 mg every 4 hours 
	- Reduced in frail/elderly patients 
	- Adjust according to response 
	- Contraindication - head injury/raised ICP
		- Early sign of cognitive dmg/increased ICP -> cognitive ability 
		- Morphine with someone who already has decreased cognitive ability -> even more difficult to assess their ACVPU
	- + Analgesics - pain relief drugs - can be opioid/non-opioid drugs (morphine is opioid)
- The WHO pain ladder
	- 5 key principles
		1. Administer analgesics orally whenever possible
		2. Analgesics should be given at regular intervals, duration and dose should be corresponding to the level of pain
		3. Analgesic dose/duration should be given according to the _patient's_ rating of the pain
		4. Dose should start at the lowest dose/duration possible but then adapted to the response
		5. Analgesics should be administered consistently for effective pain management
	- Steps
		1. Non-opioids (eg paracetamol)
		2. Weak opioids (eg codeine)
		3. Strong opioids (eg morphine, fentanyl)
		- Adjuvants - used to increase the efficacy of the main drug - can be used at each step of the ladder
			- Caffeine is an adjuvant for paracetamol and ibuprofen
- Tranexamic acid - clotting agent - improves platelet aggregation -> makes the first clot that forms better/faster

The nurse cut away Vasile’s clothing revealing his left arm. You notice that his arm appears deformed. You watch another of the doctors as she examines the patient and she talks you through her findings. She agrees that the arm is deformed. The nurse cannot feel a brachial or a radial pulse on the left and the left ulnar pulse is weak. The pulses are all easily felt on the right arm. The left hand is cooler and the capillary refill time is slower in the left than in the right. 
- Radial pulse - damage to the forearm blocking the radial artery (motorbike?)
- Ulnar/brachial pulse - some damage to the brachial artery 


The patient is unable to abduct his left arm and he is also unable to flex his arm. However this may be due to the pain which Vasile is in. He can, however, move his fingers without difficulty. Because of the injury the doctor is unable to test his tendon reflexes. The doctor tries to check the patient’s sensation over the left arm, but the patient mainly complains of pain and asks if he
will “lose his arm?” 
- Scared
- Abduct - Maybe a deltoid/supraspinatus injury - supraspinatus injury/axillary artery
- Flex - Sounds even more like a deltoid injury 
	- OR broken acromion/clavicle -> unable to contract
- I mean, pain means there're some nerves still there to conduct...

The doctor performs a secondary survey, asking you what she is looking for. No further evidence of injury is found. 
- Secondary survey - AMPLE and head to toe
	- Cognitive damage 
	- Further bleeding/rashes <- road rash 
	- Bruises/further deformities/broken bones etc
	- Medications - opioids? etc
	- Prev medical history - bones at risk, surgery, cognitive?
	- Hasn't eaten?
- No further injury 👍

The patient then goes to the x-ray department for a Trauma CT and plain-films of his arm. When he returns the doctor asks you to take a past medical, drug and social history from him. You discover that Vasile has been previously fit and well and has never been to hospital before. He uses the occasional ‘over-the-counter’ paracetamol or ibuprofen, but does not take any
regular medication. He also tells you that he is working on a farm and his partner, whom he supports, is expecting a baby in 2 months’ time. Vasile keeps asking you if he is going to lose his arm, and how much this hospital attendance is going to cost him. 
- Trauma CT and plain films
	- Whole body CT scan
		- Usually does head to pelvis, but can include limbs if indicated
		- Patient is not repositioned
		- Unstable patients - separate arterial and venous phase images are obtained -> aids in finding points to embolise (block the bleeding)
			- Phases - time after contrast is injected - shows different structures  ![[Pasted image 20260204143615.png]]
	- Plain films ![[Pasted image 20260204143944.png]]
		- Simplest xray imaging technique - not CT scan - the "typical" xray scan
		- Small dose of xrays
- Never been to hospital before - obvs freaking out then
- Cost him - eh? not NHS? 
	- Farm - maybe doesn't know - that's classist - idk ┐(ツ)┌
	- Previous healthcare experience may not have been UK based/NHS

The doctor has a look at the x-rays and says that there is a humeral shaft fracture. The Trauma CT shows no additional injuries. The Orthopaedic team attend the A&E department to review Vasile. They arrange for him to have angiography before they take him to theatre. They agree that he might have a brachial plexus injury but feel he should go to theatre first and then be
reassessed afterwards, possibly including an MRI scan.
- Humeral shaft fracture - could have hit the brachial artery but left the superior ulnar collateral artery -> ulnar recurrent arteries
	- ![[Pasted image 20260204143150.png]]
- Angiograph - check the blood vessels 
	- Eh? Trauma CT already did that no?
	- Usually done only for the torso first, then only limbs if noticed - but easy to overlook amidst the chaos 
- Address the larger concern of humeral fracture before the nerve damage
