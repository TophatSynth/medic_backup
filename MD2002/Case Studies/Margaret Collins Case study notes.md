# Scenario notes

You are on GP attachment in Cupar and go with your GP supervisor on a home visit to see Margaret Collins, a 63 year old female. You think of a few questions to ask the GP.
- GP Clinic 
- Home visit 
	- Hygeine and obstacles (thinking physio)
	- Able to observe how the patient moves and interacts in home environment
- 63 year old female
	- Risk factors 
  

The GP tells you that Margaret has been home for 2 weeks, following an uneventful right total knee replacement for long standing osteoarthritis. On discharge from hospital, Margaret was given a 14 day supply of dalteparin injections; 5000 units daily subcutaneous; and a paper information leaflet for this medication along with written post-operative care advice. Margaret had contacted the practice that morning complaining of a sore lower right leg.
- Dalteparin - anticoagulant
	- Required to give dalteparin injections to self 
	- Risk factors - elderly + risk of bleeding <- underlying conditions
- Paper information leaflet
	- Provides the patient information 
	- May not be able to read it well - need to confirm understanding verbally 
- Follow up is important, especially post surgery 
	- Higher risk if she has underlying conditions 
- Sore lower right leg
	- Indicative of underlying problem

Margaret has no other relevant medical history other than osteoarthritis. Her 67-year-old husband has been caring for her since she came out of hospital, they have no additional support at home. Margaret is a retired administrator. Since returning home she has been reluctant to weight bear and has spent much of her time sitting in her chair watching television. When taking the history from Margaret, the GP asks her about her current medication and allergies. She has no allergies, but Margaret does tell the GP that she stopped taking the dalteparin injections after 3 days as it was causing small bruises on her abdomen. She did not realise the full significance of the medication as she struggled to understand the leaflet.
- Reluctant to bear weight on knee - has the surgery gone wrong? pain control inadequate? physio? 
- Bruises - blood clotting? 
	- Dalteparin side effects - haemorrhage
	- If injecting in the same spot repeatedly - may cause more bruising
- Is husband physically fit to support her as she recovers 
- Failure of the doctor to verify understanding of instructions 
- Red flags
	- Post operative sedentary + not taking medication -> high risk of thrombus/embolism 
  

You elicit the following signs and symptoms from the patient:
GCS 15, BMI 34 (from GP records prior to surgery), short of breath at rest sitting in chair.
Pleuritic right sided chest pain
Chest examination normal
Respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. SpO2 88% (FiO2 = 0.21)
Right calf tender, hot and swollen – circumference 4cm greater than left calf.
    
- Short of breath at rest + tachypnea - pulmonary embolism 
	- 22 bpm is within normal elderly resp rate (16-25bpm) 
- GCS fine
- BMI slightly obese - may have to adjust for age - may actually be within healthy range
- Sedentary lifestyle may add to blood clot risk 
- Not enough info about lifestyle
	- May be a smoker 
- Pleuritic pain - inflammation of parietal pleura 
	- Shallower breaths to avoid pain, perhaps 
- Chest examination - ruling out trauma 
- FiO2 is fine (21% is room air oxygen%) 
	- SpO2 quite low - 88% is the high concern threshold
- Calf
	- Very likely to be DVT - swelling, heat and pain 
  

The GP suspects a venous thromboembolism (VTE) and immediately arranges for Margaret to be taken to hospital. The GP writes a short referral letter whilst waiting for the ambulance. Margaret is given oxygen in the ambulance and the paramedics insert a peripheral venous catheter (PVC).
- VTE - yep, DVT 
	- Emergency (instantaneous death) -> get to hospital quickly
	- Also dangerous SpO2 level 
- Referral letter
	- SBAR
- Peripheral venous catheter - probably administering medications, maybe more anticoagulant? 
  

Physical findings on admission to the Emergency Department (ED) include:
Vital signs as per the EWS chart
Productive cough with haemoptysis
- Haemoptysis - coughing up blood - not good 👎

  
Investigation results:
Chest x-ray is normal.
12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) shows evidence of right ventricular strain
An ultrasound scan (doppler) detects a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the right lower limb.
- ECG - ✨ pulmonary embolism ✨
	- Right ventricle pumping blood to the lungs - has to pump harder against the embolism 
- PE could have moved into a pulmonary artery now

  

A full blood count (FBC) showed the following results:

| Hb: 111 g/l            |                         |

- Hb and RBC - a lil low 
- High platelet count - hasn't been taking dalteparin 
- WBC not high - not infection 

Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis on 28% oxygen shows PaO2 = 9.7kPa
- 9.7kPa is very low for 28% O2 - hypoxia 
  

A Wells Score is calculated which suggests a pulmonary embolism (PE) is likely.
- PE -  YAY 😃✨
  

Possible investigations that were considered and discussed included: coagulation status, ventilation/perfusion scan (V/Q scan), CTPA.
- V/Q scan 
	- Assessing ventilation and vasculature of the lungs 
- CTPA
	- CT scan to look for blood clots 
  

The management plan discussion included anticoagulation (low molecular weight heparin, apixaban and warfarin were mentioned) and “clot busting”.
- Addressing various drug options
	- Heparin - IV 
	- Apixaban - oral - learned from their mistakes, changed from injections 
	- Warfarin - oral, maintenance after treatment
- Clot busting
	- Trying to explain to her what the treatment is for 
  

Margaret is admitted to the Medical Assessment Unit, as it is thought that there is a high probability of PE.

Margaret makes an excellent recovery and is discharged home 2 days later with a supply of apixaban 10mg twice daily for 7 days then 5mg apixaban twice daily, a completed alert card and information booklet. # Summary 
- Try and keep other pathologies in mind instead of zoning onto singular problem 
- Sign vs symptoms 
	- Symptoms is what patient tells you, goes into history
	- Signs is what you see, goes into examinations
- Diff bt arterial/venous thrombosis
	- Arterial - 5P's 
- PVC - what for?
	- Medication 
	- Done in the ambulance to save time once at the hospital 
- Wells score - https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/362/wells-criteria-dvt
- ABG - PaO2, PaCO2, ph, and lactate conc
- CTPA vs V/Q scan
	- V/Q used on pregnant women bc less radiation
	- CTPA - gold standard but higher radiation dose
- Anticoagulants 
	- Dalteparin is for prevention of PE/DVT 
		- Prevention usually subcutaneous injection
	- Apixaban is for treatment of PE/DVT - most commonly used one now
	- Warfarin - treatment, not used much now, bc of monitoring needed (INR) 
- D-dimer test
	- Protein that breaks down clots 
	- If elevated - indicates a clot, but can be elevated due to many reasons
